Bringing a client onto the platform is a repeatable procedure, designed so that a mistake
at any step fails closed instead of leaving a half-working organisation.

## Phase one: provision

A platform administrator provisions the organisation from the control plane. One audited
request creates three things:

<div class="defs not-content">
  <div><dt>The organisation</dt><dd>With its name, its address slug (lowercase letters, digits and single hyphens, unique across the platform) and its identity issuer.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Its default community</dt><dd>The first building.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Its first administrator</dt><dd>The person who will configure the rest. They hold the administration role and no resident-data capability.</dd></div>
</div>

The organisation starts in a **provisioning** state. A typo in the identity settings is not
permanent, since they can be edited afterwards.

## Phase two: bind identity

The administrator's account must be tied to a real identity from the client's own identity
system, and the organisation's issuer must be recorded. Staff cannot sign in until this is
done, because the platform does not run its own passwords.

## Phase three: activate

<div class="grid-cards not-content">
  <div class="panel"><h4>Gate one</h4><p>The identity issuer is bound.</p></div>
  <div class="panel"><h4>Gate two</h4><p>The default community is active.</p></div>
  <div class="panel"><h4>Gate three</h4><p>The administrator account is active.</p></div>
  <div class="panel"><h4>Gate four</h4><p>The administrator is bound to a real identity.</p></div>
</div>

The control plane shows which gates are open. Activation is refused until all four hold, and
every transition is recorded with a reason in the organisation's own audit ledger. A
platform administrator can also **repair** an organisation that has locked itself out.

## Phase four: configure

The client administrator works through the console in a sensible order:

<ol class="steps not-content">
  <li><h4>Facilities</h4><p>Add each building. Each gets its own pricing and its own residents.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Staff, roles and memberships</h4><p>Invite colleagues, assign a role, and give each person a membership in the communities they work in. A person with an identity subject is active at once. One without stays inactive until explicitly activated.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Family portal</h4><p>Set the community's name, accent colour, welcome text, contact person, and the sections and documents requested.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Communications</h4><p>Choose whether outbound messages are sent, and configure the email and text gateways, so invitations and codes can reach families.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Life-plan policy</h4><p>The client's finance leadership supplies the thresholds, rates, care duration profiles and contract catalogue. An administrator publishes them. <strong>Until thresholds are approved and published, no qualification can be completed.</strong></p></li>
  <li><h4>Community pricing</h4><p>For each building, which contracts are offered, with entrance fees and refund rates, and monthly fees for each level of care.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Review policy</h4><p>The approval chain: prerequisites, committee slots, how long a review stays valid, and the named waiver and escalation approvers.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Units and approvers</h4><p>Inventory, dated price schedules with sources, and the named people who may approve a price reduction.</p></li>
  <li><h4>Report access</h4><p>Explicit grants for the leadership who will run cross-community reports.</p></li>
</ol>

## Phase five: invite

Counselors create the first family applications. A first application is a good end-to-end
check: the invitation arrives, the family can complete and submit, and staff can review and
promote.

## Things that commonly go wrong

<div class="defs not-content">
  <div><dt>Staff cannot sign in</dt><dd>Almost always an unclosed gate: the issuer, the administrator's identity binding, or the account not yet active. The control plane shows which.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>A person signs in and sees nothing</dt><dd>They have a role but no membership in any building. Membership is what opens resident data.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>A projection is unconfigured</dt><dd>No approved thresholds have been published. The outcome stays unconfigured until they are.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Families never receive the code</dt><dd>The email or text gateway has not been configured, or outbound messages are switched off. See <a href="/operations/runbook/">Support runbook</a>.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>A reduction cannot be approved</dt><dd>The approver list is empty. It is empty by default on purpose.</dd></div>
</div>

## Starting a production environment safely

Before any of this, the platform itself must start, and it is built to refuse to start
badly. In a production setting it will not start if it finds:

<div class="split not-content">
  <div class="panel panel--stop">
    <h4>Development secrets</h4>
    <p>Any encryption key, session key, signing key or audit-chain seed that still has its development default, or is too short.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="panel panel--stop">
    <h4>The wrong database identities</h4>
    <p>The application or the portal not connecting as its own restricted identity.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="panel panel--stop">
    <h4>Insecure endpoints</h4>
    <p>An identity issuer, key set, portal address or allowed origin that is not secure, or an empty origin list.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="panel panel--stop">
    <h4>Development shortcuts</h4>
    <p>The development sign-in stand-in switched on.</p>
  </div>
</div>

A refusal to start is a feature. A platform that boots with a development key is a platform
whose data is protected by a key everyone knows.
