The platform decides **who may publish** a setting and makes sure two people cannot
overwrite one another. It cannot decide whether the numbers are right. That is a matter of
the client's own governance, and this page describes what good governance looks like around
the controls the product provides.

## What the product guarantees

<div class="defs not-content">
  <div><dt>Only the right authority publishes</dt><dd>Central policy, pricing, review policy, price approvers, portal settings and report access are all administrator functions. Ordinary staff cannot change them.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Every publication is a version</dt><dd>Nothing is edited in place. Earlier versions remain, so the history of every assumption is complete.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Concurrent edits conflict</dt><dd>An editor sends the revision it loaded. If someone published first, the change is refused and the administrator reviews. See <a href="/interfaces/concurrency/">Revisions and idempotency</a>.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Published changes are audited</dt><dd>With who, what and when, in the client's own ledger.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>History is protected</dt><dd>A qualification pins the exact policy and pricing it used, so a later change cannot alter it. Quotes and approvals that depended on a changed setting go stale and are refused.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Nothing is supplied</dt><dd>The product ships no approved financial values. An empty policy is reported as empty.</dd></div>
</div>

## A change process worth having

The product gives every change a version and an author. A client's process turns that into
accountability. A sound one, in practice:

<ol class="steps not-content">
  <li>
    <h4>Classify the change</h4>
    <p><strong>Outcome-moving</strong>: thresholds, growth rates, care durations, conventions, fee schedules. <strong>Structural</strong>: the contract catalogue, the review chain, approvers. <strong>Cosmetic</strong>: portal wording, labels. Each tier warrants different approval.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h4>Get the owner's approval in writing</h4>
    <p>Finance leadership for anything outcome-moving. The product records who published, not who agreed. The agreement should live somewhere the client keeps it.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h4>Project known cases first</h4>
    <p>Keep a small set of representative households, including marginal ones, and project them before and after. The workspace lets a reviewer keep a baseline and compare an alternative, and warns when the two used different configuration versions.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h4>Publish at a quiet time</h4>
    <p>A published central change makes in-flight quotes and reviews stale. Publishing between busy periods avoids sending reviewers back to re-project mid-case.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h4>Tell the people it affects</h4>
    <p>Reviewers will see conflicts and refreshed assumptions. A note beforehand turns a surprise into a routine.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h4>Record the version label</h4>
    <p>Every policy carries a human-readable version label. Use a convention that says when and why, so a qualification's pinned version is meaningful to a human years later.</p>
  </li>
</ol>

## Changes that need extra care

<div class="defs not-content">
  <div><dt>Thresholds</dt><dd>The floors move marginal households between outcomes. A threshold change is a business policy decision. Past qualifications are unaffected, and new ones follow the new floors.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>The refund convention</dt><dd>Whether the contingent refund counts towards thresholds changes the classification of marginal households without changing any figure. Treat it as a threshold-level decision.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>The return convention</dt><dd>Monthly or annual crediting changes balances. Prefer the default for new communities.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>The contract catalogue</dt><dd>Publishing a central change checks community assignments that depend on it, so an edit cannot silently invalidate a community's pricing. Expect the check to refuse an edit that would.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>The review chain</dt><dd>Publishing a new effective policy invalidates earlier completions and approvals on open cases, which is correct and may surprise reviewers.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Approvers</dt><dd>Changing the approver list makes pending price reductions need re-requesting. It does not affect completed qualifications.</dd></div>
</div>

## Undoing a mistake

Because nothing is overwritten, undoing a change is simple:

<div class="split not-content">
  <div class="panel panel--ok">
    <h4>Publish the earlier values again</h4>
    <p>The earlier version is still there. Publishing its values creates a new, later version with the old numbers. The mistaken version remains in history.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="panel panel--stop">
    <h4>There is no deletion</h4>
    <p>A published version cannot be removed, and a qualification already made on it keeps it. That is the point.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Qualifications completed under a mistaken version are not repaired by reverting. If a
decision depends on one, requalify it. The earlier snapshot stays as evidence of what was
believed at the time.

## Keeping a record outside the product

The audit trail names who published and when. It does not carry the rationale. Keep, in the
client's own records: the approval, the reason, the cases projected before and after, and
the version label that resulted. Together with the audit trail, that is a complete account.
